01

The problem

On a flat screen, you can see left, right, up and down directly. Near and far you have to guess, from clues like size, perspective and shadows. People who grew up with 3D games read those clues without thinking. People who didn't, often can't.

I've never been taught to draw, and I still taught myself to model a 3D character in Blender. I've also taught girls in Kano who had never touched a laptop. If XR and 3D tools ever reach people like them, they will meet this problem with no training at all, in tools designed by people who already think in 3D.

So I built a tiny experiment to ask one question: can a simple visual cue close that gap? It builds on research showing that simple, non-intrusive visual guides help people draw more accurately in VR, whatever their spatial ability.

02

What I'm testing

Four hypotheses, each updated as new results arrive.

03

Designing the experiment

Every design decision had to answer two questions: is it fair, and can anyone take part?

The task

Move an orange cube to the exact spot of a teal ball in a furnished room, then press PLACE. Two rounds of five targets.

Why: one simple action isolates depth judgement from everything else.

The guides

A thin pole from each object to the floor, and a matching "shadow" disc underneath.

Why: they turn a hard depth question into an easy floor-position question, without blocking the view.

No real shadows

I switched off the scene's real-time lighting shadows.

Why: shadows are themselves a depth cue. Leaving them on would quietly help the "no guides" round.

Fixed targets

Every participant gets the same ten target positions.

Why: differences come from the person and the condition, not luck.

Order by coin flip

The game randomly decides whether guides come first or second.

Why: people improve with practice, so a fixed order would favour the second round.

Error split three ways

Each placement records error in depth, side and height separately, plus time and target distance.

Why: a single "total error" would hide where people actually go wrong.

Phone-first

A web build with on-screen buttons. No install: anyone with a link can take part.

Why: the people I care about most are less likely to own a headset or a gaming PC.

Their own keyboard

Name entry opens the phone's native keyboard through a small browser plugin.

Why: Unity's built-in text box doesn't open phone keyboards in the browser.

How it felt

Players report their 3D experience first, and which round felt easier at the end.

Why: comparing feelings with numbers often reveals the most interesting design problems.
04

How it evolved

v1

A bare prototype

A cube, a sphere and a floor, with guides toggled by a key. It worked, but felt like an exercise with nobody in it.

v2

A real place

A warm room with walls, a window and furniture, a small character who cheers good placements, and touch buttons so people could play on their phones.

v3

An actual experiment

Fixed targets, counterbalanced order, per-axis error, experience and comfort questions, and a results screen participants can screenshot.

Fix

The pink screen

On the first phone test, everything rendered bright pink: the web build had stripped the shader my objects needed. Linking a real material asset kept it in the build.

05

Who took part

Participants open the link on their own phone, play both rounds (about five minutes) and send back a screenshot of their results with a short voice note on what made it hard. Names are replaced with IDs (P1, P2…) on this page.

1

Depth is the hard direction

Without guides, the experienced players were far more wrong about near and far than about left, right, up or down. On screen, the cube probably looked lined up with the ball while actually sitting metres in front of or behind it, and several of these placements took under five seconds.

2

The guides nearly erased it

With guides on, depth error fell for every participant, in two cases by over 95%. Height error dropped less, which suggests the pole shows where an object is better than how high it is.

3

Nobody felt the difference

Every participant so far said both rounds felt about the same, despite very different accuracy. Without guides, people don't know they're wrong. For design, that matters: if users can't feel an error, they won't ask for help.

4

A surprise about experience

The one participant without 3D experience was the most accurate, even without guides, by moving slowly (about 34 seconds per target). The experienced players placed in seconds and were confidently wrong. Speed and confidence, not experience, may be the real story.

07

Honest limits

  • Tiny sample. These are early trends from a handful of people, not proof.
  • Order effect. So far, every participant happened to play without guides first, so practice may explain part of the improvement. Next, I'll alternate the order on purpose.
  • Different target sets. Each round uses its own five targets, so target difficulty may differ slightly between conditions.
  • Different phones. Screen size and touch accuracy vary between devices.
  • Informal pilot. This is design research for learning, without formal ethics approval, so results won't be published as a study.
08

What's next

  • More first-timers. Run it with Tech Sis Diaries girls and older adults, the people this question is really about.
  • Balanced order. Alternate who starts with guides, so practice can't explain the results.
  • Show people their error. Test whether instant feedback ("you were 2 m too close") changes how people judge depth, given that nobody felt the difference.
  • Compare cues. Try a floor grid, a depth number and a soft glow against the pole and shadow.
  • Bring it into VR. Run the same task in a headset, where stereo vision adds another depth cue.

Be part of it

This is a living study. Anyone can take part, whatever their age, background or experience with 3D, and every new person makes the picture clearer. People with no 3D experience are especially welcome.
